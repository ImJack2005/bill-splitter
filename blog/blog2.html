Tipping Etiquette Across US States: What You Should Actually Leave

📅 June 2026 · ⏱ 6 min read

You're sitting at a restaurant in Portland, Oregon. The meal was great, the service was attentive, and now you're staring at a check with no suggested tip amounts on it — and you realize you have no idea what the local expectation actually is. Is 18% enough? Is 20% the floor? Does the no-sales-tax thing change anything?

Tipping in the US is one of those things that everyone does but nobody fully agrees on. The amount, the calculation method, what counts as "good service" — it all varies more than most people realize, and it quietly shifts depending on which state you're in.

Here's everything you need to know, broken down by region and state.

First: How Tipping Actually Works in the US

Unlike most countries, the US restaurant industry is built around tipped wages. In many states, restaurants are legally allowed to pay servers a lower base wage — sometimes as low as $2.13/hour federally — with the expectation that tips will bring them to or above minimum wage. In practice, tips are not optional generosity. They are how servers pay their rent.

That context matters when you're deciding whether 15% is "fine." In most situations, it isn't.

Quick baseline

The current US standard for sit-down restaurant service is 18% for adequate service, 20% for good service, and 22–25% for excellent service. Anything below 15% is widely read as a signal of dissatisfaction, not thriftiness.

Does Your State's Tax Rate Affect the Tip?

Yes — indirectly. The convention in the US is to tip on your pre-tax subtotal, not the final bill. So the tax rate itself doesn't change your tip amount. But it does change how you read the check.

In a high-tax city like New York (8.875% combined), your final bill can look 30–35% higher than your menu total by the time tax and tip are added. In a no-tax state like Oregon, that same meal might only run 20–22% over menu price. If you're not accounting for this, you can easily over- or under-tip without meaning to.

Common mistake

Some people tip on the total line — the number after tax is already added. This means you're tipping on the tax itself, which slightly over-tips. Not a huge deal, but worth knowing.

Others use the "double the tax" shortcut in high-tax cities. In NYC, double the 8.875% tax ≈ 17.75% tip. It's fast but lands on the low side of acceptable.

The No-Sales-Tax States: Does That Change Anything?

Five US states have no statewide sales tax: Oregon, Montana, New Hampshire, Delaware, and Alaska. If you're dining in these states, your menu price is almost exactly what you'll pay before tip — which makes the math simpler but doesn't change what you should tip.

A common mistake visitors make is thinking "I saved money on tax, so I can tip a bit less." This misunderstands how tipping works. Your tip is based on the server's effort and the value of the service — not on what the state charges for sales tax. The server's wage is the same regardless.

In no-tax states: Your $60 meal stays at $60 before tip. Tip 20% = $12. Total = $72.

In NYC: That same $60 meal becomes $65.33 after tax. Tip 20% on pre-tax = $12. Total = $77.33.

The difference is in what you pay, not in what the server earns from you.

When to Tip More Than 20%

The 20% baseline is just that — a baseline. There are situations where tipping higher is the right call:

  • Large groups. Managing a table of eight or more is significantly harder than a table of two. If the service held up, reflect that.
  • Long stays. If you lingered for three hours over a bottle of wine, you occupied a table that could have turned over twice. A higher tip acknowledges that.
  • Small orders, full service. If you only ordered coffee and dessert but got full table service, tip on the effort, not the bill size. A 30–40% tip on a $12 order is still just $3.60.
  • Bad weather or unusual circumstances. If you're dining out during a snowstorm or a holiday, the staff showed up so you could eat out. That deserves recognition.

When Is It Okay to Tip Less?

Genuine service failures — an order that never arrived, a long unexplained wait, repeated mistakes that weren't corrected — are legitimate reasons to reduce a tip. But "I didn't love the food" isn't. The kitchen sets the menu; the server brings it to you.

If something went wrong, the more effective move is to mention it to a manager. Many restaurants will comp a dish or offer a discount — which matters more to the restaurant than a reduced tip matters to the server.

The Honest Summary

Tipping norms vary across the US, but not as wildly as people sometimes claim. The honest baseline for sit-down restaurant service is 18–20% essentially everywhere. A few high-cost cities push that floor toward 22–25%. A handful of rural areas accept 15%. But the middle ground — 20% on your pre-tax subtotal — is a safe, respectful number in virtually every state.

The bigger variable isn't geography. It's the situation: the size of your group, how long you stayed, what went right or wrong. Use the state norms as a baseline and adjust from there.
